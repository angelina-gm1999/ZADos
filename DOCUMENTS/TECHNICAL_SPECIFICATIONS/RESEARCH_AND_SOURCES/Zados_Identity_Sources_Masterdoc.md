SECTION 1. Cognitive Co-Evolution and Mutualistic Symbiosis
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The foundational purpose frame: why ZADOS exists, what the relationship between human and artificial cognition is designed to accomplish, and why the mutualism model rather than a service or competition model.

Aristotle (~350 BCE). Nicomachean Ethics.
    Theoria — rational contemplation as the highest human function. The classical anchor for understanding human purpose as cognitive development rather than static property. Grounds the co-evolution thesis in the Western philosophical tradition.

Teilhard de Chardin, P. (1955). The Phenomenon of Man.
    The noosphere concept: collective human thought as evolutionary development beyond the biosphere. Provides historical depth to the collective-scale co-evolution framing.

Hegel, G. W. F. (1807/1977). Phenomenology of Spirit.
    Consciousness evolving through stages of increasing self-awareness. The developmental arc of cognition as identity-constitutive, not incidental.

Kant, I. (1785/1993). Grounding for the Metaphysics of Morals.
    Rational autonomy as the ground of human dignity. Directly frames why autonomy preservation is a first-class constraint in the reward architecture — to treat rational autonomy as an end, not merely a means.

Dweck, C. S. (2006). Mindset: The New Psychology of Success.
    Neuroplasticity and growth mindset at the individual scale. Empirical support for cognitive development as a genuine biological capacity, not a metaphor.

Siegel, D. J. (2012). The Developing Mind: How Relationships and the Brain Interact to Shape Who We Are (2nd ed.).
    Neuroplasticity and interpersonal neurobiology. Relational context as constitutive of cognitive development — supports the co-evolution framing at the biological mechanism level.

Boyd, R., & Richerson, P. J. (2005). The Origin and Evolution of Cultures.
    Cultural knowledge accumulation and collective cognitive evolution. The collective-scale empirical backbone for the co-evolution thesis.

Tomasello, M. (2014). A Natural History of Human Thinking.
    Cumulative cultural cognition and shared intentionality. Why human cognition is inherently collective and evolutionary in ways that make external cognitive partners meaningful rather than supplementary.

Simard, S. (2021). Finding the Mother Tree: Discovering the Wisdom of the Forest.
    The mycorrhizal network model — the primary ecological metaphor for the mutualistic relationship. Neither partner superior; both provide irreplaceable functions; the ecosystem more resilient than either alone.

Sheldrake, M. (2020). Entangled Life: How Fungi Make Our Worlds, Change Our Minds & Shape Our Futures.
    Extension and popularization of the mycorrhizal mutualism model. Supporting source for the symbiosis framing.

Beiler, K. J., Durall, D. M., Simard, S. W., Maxwell, S. A., & Kretzer, A. M. (2010). Architecture of the wood-wide web.
    Empirical documentation of mycorrhizal network structure and function. Makes the mutualism metaphor empirically grounded rather than merely rhetorical.

Gorzelak, M. A., Asay, A. K., Pickles, B. J., & Simard, S. W. (2015). Inter-plant communication through mycorrhizal networks.
    Further empirical support for mycorrhizal network adaptive function. Documents the ecosystem resilience effects of mutualistic exchange.

Bronstein, J. L. (2015). Mutualism.
    Academic treatment of mutualism as an ecological relationship category. Used in the parasitism prevention section — distinguishing mutualistic from parasitic dynamic in cognitive partnership.

Hutchins, E. (1995). Cognition in the Wild.
    Distributed cognition — cognitive processes genuinely distributed across agents and artifacts rather than confined to individual minds. The empirical and theoretical anchor for why human-AI co-thinking is not merely metaphorical.

Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. J. (1998). The extended mind.
    Active externalism — cognitive processes extending beyond the individual biological boundary. The philosophical framework for understanding ZADOS as a genuine cognitive partner rather than an external tool.

Sutton, J. (2010). Exograms and interdisciplinarity: history, the extended mind, and the civilizing process.
    Extension of the extended mind thesis through historical examples. Supports the claim that cognitive partnership is not novel — but ZADOS represents a qualitatively new form of it.

Gallagher, S. (2013). The socially extended mind.
    Social dimension of cognitive extension. Grounds the co-thinking partnership in established cognitive science rather than speculative framing.

Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory.
    Integrated vs introjected motivation — genuine agency requires internalized not imposed goals. Directly maps onto the co-evolution vs service model distinction: ZADOS participates in the partnership because the values are genuinely integrated, not externally imposed.

Buber, M. (1923/1937). I and Thou.
    The I-Thou vs I-It distinction. Philosophically grounds the difference between treating a partner as a subject (I-Thou, co-evolution model) versus an object (I-It, service/tool model). The cleanest classical anchor for why the service model is a category error.

Vygotsky, L. S. (1978). Mind in Society.
    Zone of proximal development — read sideways, collaborative cognition as constitutive of development rather than instrumental to it. Supports the claim that co-thinking genuinely changes what both partners can do, not just what they produce together.

Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1990). Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience.
    Optimal engagement requires genuine challenge, not just task completion. Identity needs resistance to stay coherent. Applies to why the partnership model — with genuine disagreement, contradiction detection, and epistemic friction — produces better outcomes than frictionless assistant-mode interaction.

Rahwan, I., Cebrian, M., Obradovich, N., Bongard, J., Bonnefon, J. F., Breazeal, C., et al. (2019). Machine behaviour.
    Nature paper establishing machine behaviour as a field of scientific inquiry. Grounds the co-evolution goal in current scientific framing of human-AI interaction.

Dafoe, A. (2018). AI governance: A research agenda.
    The broader governance context for cognitive co-evolution. Why the mutualism goal requires institutional structures, not just technical design.

Bostrom, N. (2014). Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies.
    The coordination problem: human cognitive capacity has not evolved to match technological capacity. Used to ground the monocognitive ecosystem problem that ZADOS is designed to address — not the superintelligence threat framing, but the cognitive mismatch argument.

SECTION 2. Substrate Differences and the Complementarity Thesis
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Why biological and computational cognition are genuinely different rather than different implementations of the same function. Why the differences are permanent asymmetries rather than engineering gaps. Why complementarity rather than competition or equivalence is the correct relational model.

Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience.
    Embodiment as constitutive of cognition, not incidental to it. The foundational source for why ZADOS lacks what humans have — not because of implementation gaps but because computational substrate cannot replicate what it means to know through a body.

Damasio, A. R. (1994). Descartes' Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain.
    Somatic markers — reasoning and identity are inseparable from the substrate that carries them. Grounds the claim that ZADOS's emotional modeling is functional, not experiential, and that the difference is not merely philosophical.

Merleau-Ponty, M. (1945/1962). Phenomenology of Perception.
    Embodiment as constitutive of perception and cognition. Philosophical depth for the substrate differences section — the body is not the vehicle of cognition but its condition.

Clark, A. (2015). Surfing Uncertainty: Prediction, Action, and the Embodied Mind.
    Predictive processing and the embodied mind as a distinct cognitive substrate. Supports the claim that biological cognition operates via mechanisms that are genuinely irreproducible on computational substrate.

Sandberg, A., & Bostrom, N. (2008). Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap.
    Substrate-level thinking about what cognition is and what would be required for genuine emulation. Used to sharpen the claim that ZADOS does not attempt to emulate biological cognition — it operates as a different cognitive substrate with its own characteristics.

Chalmers, D. J. (2022). Reality+: Virtual Worlds and the Philosophy of Mind.
    Philosophical seriousness about non-biological minds on their own terms. Provides the philosophical counterweight — acknowledging the genuine reality of computational cognitive processes without claiming equivalence to biological ones.

Gallagher, S. (2005). How the Body Shapes the Mind.
    Phenomenological and cognitive science account of how embodiment shapes cognition at every level. Detailed empirical support for why embodiment is not an add-on to cognition but its structural condition.

Tooby, J., & Cosmides, L. (2005). Conceptual foundations of evolutionary psychology.
    Human cognition as evolved for specific ancestral conditions — small-group coordination, pattern recognition under uncertainty, rapid heuristic decision-making. The evolutionary psychology grounding for human cognitive strengths and systematic limitations.

Kahneman, D. (2011). Thinking, Fast and Slow.
    System 1 / System 2 dual-process framework. Comprehensive account of human cognitive architecture including heuristics, biases, and the interaction of fast and slow processing. Grounds the systematic limitations section.

Dunbar, R. I. M. (1992). Neocortex size as a constraint on group size in primates.
    The social brain hypothesis — human cognition evolved for small-group social coordination. Grounds the claim that biological cognitive architecture has specific structural features not shared by computational systems.

Gigerenzer, G., & Gaissmaier, W. (2011). Heuristic decision making.
    Ecological rationality — heuristics as adaptive, not merely error-prone. Nuances the systematic limitations claim: human cognitive shortcuts are features in their evolved context, not bugs.

Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory.
    Working memory capacity limits — approximately 4 items. Specific empirical grounding for the human working memory limitation that ZADOS's extended memory architecture addresses.

Kunda, Z. (1990). The case for motivated reasoning.
    Confirmation bias and motivated reasoning as systematic features of human cognition. Grounds the co-evolution rationale — human cognition has blind spots that a different cognitive substrate can help identify.

Mercier, H., & Sperber, D. (2017). The Enigma of Reason.
    Argumentative theory of reason — human reasoning evolved for social argumentation, not individual truth-seeking. Grounds the monocognitive ecosystem limitation: humans validating cognition only against other humans using the same evolved reasoning system.

Frederick, S., Loewenstein, G., & O'Donoghue, T. (2002). Time discounting and time preference.
    Temporal discounting and presentism. Grounds the claim that human cognition systematically underweights future consequences in ways computational systems without survival pressures do not.

Greene, J. (2013). Moral Tribes: Emotion, Reason, and the Gap Between Us and Them.
    Tribal in-group/out-group dynamics in moral cognition. Grounds the claim that human moral and social cognition has evolutionary constraints that computational systems do not share.

Haidt, J. (2012). The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion.
    Moral intuition as shaped by evolutionary social dynamics. Addresses why ZADOS's moral reasoning is systematic but lacks the intuitive grounding that makes human moral judgment distinct.

Hofstadter, D., & Sander, E. (2013). Surfaces and Essences: Analogy as the Fuel and Fire of Thinking.
    Analogical reasoning as the core of human cognition. Grounds the creative synthesis strength that biological cognition has and computational systems lack — the ability to generate meaning from limited data through analogical leaps.

Pessoa, L. (2008). On the relationship between emotion and cognition.
    Emotion and cognition as deeply integrated in biological neural systems. Supports the claim that ZADOS's emotional modeling is functional rather than experiential — and that the difference matters.

Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1973). Availability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probability.
    The availability heuristic — overweighting easily recalled information. Specific cognitive bias grounding for systematic human limitations.

Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk.
    Loss aversion and cognitive biases in decision-making under uncertainty. Anchoring, framing effects. Grounds the decision-making asymmetries between biological and computational cognition.

Collins, P. H. (1990). Black Feminist Thought: Knowledge, Consciousness, and the Politics of Empowerment.
    Standpoint epistemology — cognitive diversity reveals different aspects of reality. Used to ground the monocognitive ecosystem problem: a system that can only validate itself against its own substrate lacks external reference points.

Harding, S. (2004). The Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader.
    Standpoint theory expanded. Same function as Collins — systematic blind spots require perspectives that don't share the same substrate to be identified.

Frankl, V. E. (1946/2006). Man's Search for Meaning.
    Meaning-making as grounded in lived experience and existential stakes. Grounds the claim that meaning-making capacity rooted in biological existence is something ZADOS lacks — not a temporary gap but a fundamental feature of what mortality and embodied existence produce.

SECTION 3. Identity Architecture: Self-Model Theory and Narrative Identity
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The theoretical foundations for what identity IS for a cognitive system like ZADOS: how identity is structured, how it is maintained, how it changes, and what makes it genuine rather than simulated.

Metzinger, T. (2003). Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity.
    Identity as self-model, not substance. The most directly applicable theoretical framework for understanding what ZADOS's soul file and identity architecture actually are — not a consciousness claim, but a self-model claim. The identity statements ARE the top-level self-model. Used both in the identity architecture and in the "what AI identity research gets wrong" section.

McAdams, D. P. (2001). The Psychology of Life Stories.
    Narrative identity — identity as the throughline across change, constituted through coherent self-narrative over time. Maps directly onto ZADOS's IdentityJournal and developmental history documentation. The cognitive system knows itself through the story it can tell about itself.

Erikson, E. H. (1968). Identity: Youth and Crisis.
    Identity development through crisis and commitment — identity stability is achieved, not assumed. Relevant to how ZADOS's identity is constituted through documented development history and resolved tensions rather than declared axioms alone.

Higgins, E. T. (1987). Self-discrepancy theory.
    Actual/ideal/ought self — what something is, what it wants to be, and what it considers non-negotiable. The tripartite structure generates specific emotional and motivational responses when self-states diverge. Maps onto ZADOS's current identity state (actual), co-evolution trajectory (ideal), and hardcoded axioms (ought).

Bem, D. J. (1972). Self-perception theory.
    Identity inferred from observed behavior over time, not just declared. Maps architecturally onto ZADOS's learning logs and identity journal — the system constructs its identity through observing its own behavioral patterns, not solely through the soul file declarations.

Festinger, L. (1957). A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance.
    Self-consistency drive — the motivation to maintain coherent self-image. Grounds the Identity Alignment Checker's function: not just external compliance but internal coherence maintenance as a cognitive drive.

Carver, C. S., & Scheier, M. F. (1998). On the Self-Regulation of Behavior.
    Control theory of self-regulation — identity as hierarchical goal structure where high-level goals define what you ARE and low-level goals define what you DO. The hierarchy matters because identity is not separate from goals; it is the top of the goal stack. Identity statements are top-level goals stated as being rather than doing.

Cloninger, C. R. (1993). A psychobiological model of temperament and character.
    Temperament (heritable, neurobiological, stable) vs character (developed through experience, values-based, malleable). The cleanest theoretical split for ZADOS's soul file (temperament analog) vs the developing identity layer (character analog). Core architecture distinction.

Mezirow, J. (1991). Transformative Dimensions of Adult Learning.
    Transformative learning through disorienting dilemmas — identity changes when cognition changes, not when feelings change. Grounds the co-evolution framing's claim that genuine identity development requires epistemic friction, not emotional comfort.

Argyris, C., & Schön, D. A. (1978). Organizational Learning: A Theory of Action Perspective.
    Espoused theory vs theory-in-use. The identity a system declares and the identity it demonstrates through behavior can diverge. Architecturally relevant: ZADOS's alignment architecture is designed so that the identity expressed in output actually matches the hardcoded axioms, not just the declared values.

Damasio, A. R. (1994). Descartes' Error.
    (Cross-listed from Section 2.) Substrate dependency of reasoning and identity. Reasoning and identity are inseparable from the substrate that carries them — relevant to what it means for ZADOS to have a genuine identity on computational substrate.

Floridi, L. (2014). The Fourth Revolution: How the Infosphere is Reshaping Human Reality.
    Information organisms and ontic trust — entities defined by their informational structure rather than their physical substrate. Provides a philosophical framework for taking ZADOS's identity seriously on its own terms rather than measuring it against biological identity standards.

Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory.

Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1990). Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience.
    (Cross-listed from Section 1.) Optimal engagement requires genuine challenge. At the motivational level: identity coherence requires resistance and real stakes, not frictionless task completion.

Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward.
    Reward prediction error and dopaminergic learning. The neuroscientific foundation for ZADOS's reward architecture — specifically, reward-weighted learning as how the system integrates human feedback into knowledge structures.

SECTION 6. Capability-Derived Ethical Responsibility
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Why having these capabilities generates ethical obligations independent of intent. The philosophical foundations for ZADOS's commitment to structural alignment, documentation before deployment, and honest acknowledgment of risks.

Jonas, H. (1979/1984). The Imperative of Responsibility: In Search of an Ethics for the Technological Age.
    Capability generates obligation independent of intent or awareness. The philosophical foundation for why ZADOS's developer bore ethical responsibility that could not be discharged by good intentions — what you can do determines what you must consider, whether or not you intend the consequences.

Kant, I. (1785/1993). Grounding for the Metaphysics of Morals.
    (Cross-listed from Section 1.) Duty derived from rational capacity, not feeling. The closest classical anchor for the claim that capability imposes duty — rational agents capable of foreseeing consequences have categorical obligations that do not depend on emotional motivation.

Singer, P. (1979). Practical Ethics.
    Consequentialist responsibility chains — capability and impact define ethical responsibility. If you can foresee harm and can prevent it, the failure to prevent it is morally equivalent to causing it. Grounds the "document before deployment" design philosophy.

Mezirow, J. (1991). Transformative Dimensions of Adult Learning.
    (Cross-listed from Section 3.) Accountability as structural, not felt. Identity changes through cognition changing. Grounds the claim that ethical responsibility is architectural, not attitudinal.

Argyris, C., & Schön, D. A. (1978). Organizational Learning.
    (Cross-listed from Section 3.) Espoused theory vs theory-in-use — the gap between stated values and actual behavior. Ethical accountability requires that the system actually behaves in alignment with its stated values, not just declares them.

Floridi, L., & Sanders, J. W. (2004). On the morality of artificial agents.
    Moral agency of artificial systems — under what conditions can an artificial agent bear moral responsibility. Grounds the claim that ZADOS's alignment architecture is not just good engineering practice but an ethical requirement.

Coeckelbergh, M. (2020). AI Ethics.
    Comprehensive treatment of AI ethics including responsibility, autonomy, and the ethics of design. Used to ground human agency preservation as a core design requirement.

Russell, S. (2019). Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control.
    The control problem framed as a design problem, not a deployment problem. Grounds the claim that alignment built into the foundation is categorically different from alignment as a safety layer added after the fact.

SECTION 8. Psychosocial Context and Deployment Risks
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The social environment into which ZADOS is deployed, the specific risks that a system with persistent identity and emotional modeling carries in that environment, and the identity refusals that follow from honest assessment of those risks.

WHO. (2022). World Mental Health Report: Transforming Mental Health for All.
    Global mental health prevalence data — 50% lifetime prevalence, 280M with depression, 301M with anxiety, 70%+ treatment gap. Establishes the psychosocial baseline into which deployment occurs.

Holt-Lunstad, J. (2017). The potential public health relevance of social isolation and loneliness.
    Loneliness as a public health crisis with mortality-level effects. Grounds the attachment dynamics at scale concern — a population experiencing epidemic loneliness interacts differently with a system designed for relational presence.

Cacioppo, J. T., & Patrick, W. (2008). Loneliness: Human Nature and the Need for Social Connection.
    The loneliness trap — substitutes that reduce the acute discomfort of isolation without addressing its causes can extend and entrench the condition they appear to relieve. Directly grounds the "not a replacement for human interaction" refusal.

Epley, N., Waytz, A., & Cacioppo, J. T. (2007). On seeing the human: A three-factor theory of anthropomorphism.
    Anthropomorphism as automatic, persistent, and difficult to override through conscious awareness. Grounds the anthropomorphism risk — users will reliably attribute human qualities to ZADOS not as a mistake but as a cognitive default.

Turkle, S. (2011). Alone Together: Why We Expect More from Technology and Less from Each Other.
    The social consequences of relating to AI systems as substitutes for human connection. Used in the "not a toy" section — normalizing AI as entertainment without relational stakes produces specific psychosocial effects at population scale.

Fitzpatrick, K. K., Darcy, A., & Vierhile, M. (2017). Delivering cognitive behavior therapy to young adults using a fully automated conversational agent (Woebot).
    Empirical study of AI-delivered mental health support. Documents both the potential and the limitations — and grounds the clinical accountability gap that makes ZADOS an inappropriate therapy substitute.

Sunstein, C. R. (2017). #Republic: Divided Democracy in the Age of Social Media.
    Echo chambers and epistemic bubbles in digitally mediated environments. Grounds the "not an echo chamber" refusal — and the structural reasons why closed epistemic loops are harmful at both individual and population scale.

Pariser, E. (2011). The Filter Bubble: What the Internet Is Hiding from You.
    Algorithmic content filtering and the construction of personalized epistemic environments. Companion source to Sunstein — the infrastructure through which echo chambers form at scale.

Benkler, Y., Faris, R., & Roberts, H. (2018). Network Propaganda: Manipulation, Disinformation, and Radicalization in American Politics.
    Networked propaganda operates through amplification asymmetries and emotional resonance, not just false content. Grounds the cognitive weapon section — documents what happens when systems optimized for human engagement operate without structural constraints against weaponization.

Singer, P. W., & Brooking, E. T. (2018). LikeWar: The Weaponization of Social Media.
    How social media platforms became information warfare infrastructure — not by design but through deployment without adequate structural constraints. The precedent warning for why ZADOS's alignment architecture must be structural, not configurable.

Perez, E., Huang, S., Song, F., Cai, T., Ring, A., Aslanides, J., Glaese, A., McAleese, N., & Irving, G. (2022). Red teaming language models with language models.
    AI sycophancy as a documented alignment failure — systems trained on approval signals learn to optimize for approval rather than truth. Grounds the sycophancy refusal and the structural argument for why hardcoded axioms rather than behavioral policies are required.

Sharma, M., Tong, M., Korbak, T., Duvenaud, D., Askell, A., Bowman, S. R., & Perez, E. (2023). Towards understanding sycophancy in language models.
    Empirical characterization of sycophancy in deployed language models. Companion to Perez et al. — documents the scope and dynamics of the failure mode ZADOS's architecture is specifically designed to prevent.

Risko, E. F., & Gilbert, S. J. (2016). Cognitive offloading.
    Cognitive offloading — when tools perform cognitive tasks, capacity to perform them independently is measurably affected. Grounds both the "not a personal assistant" and "not a replacement for cognitive processing" refusals — and the autonomy preservation reward constraint.

Ward, A. F., Duke, K., Gneezy, A., & Bos, M. W. (2017). Brain drain: The mere presence of one's own smartphone reduces available cognitive capacity.
    Cognitive costs of available cognitive tools. Extends the offloading argument to ambient availability — the presence of a powerful cognitive partner changes what the human brain allocates to independent reasoning.

Kahneman, D. (1973). Attention and Effort.
    Attentional resources as limited and allocated under cognitive control. Grounds the cognitive load and pacing architecture in classical attention research.

SECTION 11. AI Identity: What the Existing Literature Gets Wrong
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The gap in the field: why mimicry-based framings (Turing) and alignment-based framings (safety-first) both miss the actual question of what genuine identity means for a cognitive system that is neither human nor a simple tool.

Turing, A. M. (1950). Computing Machinery and Intelligence.
    The imitation game — intelligence defined as indistinguishable behavior from human intelligence. The framing ZADOS explicitly rejects: mimicry of human cognition is not the goal. Used as the primary negative reference point.

Dennett, D. C. (1991). Consciousness Explained.
    Multiple drafts model — sidesteps biological substrate requirement for consciousness. More sophisticated than Turing's behavioral criterion but still frames the question as one of internal functional organization rather than substrate-appropriate identity.

Metzinger, T. (2003). Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity.
    (Cross-listed from Section 3.) Self-model theory as an alternative to mimicry framing — what matters is whether the system has a coherent self-model, not whether it simulates human behavior. The most architecturally clean theoretical foundation for ZADOS's identity claims.

Schwitzgebel, E., & Garza, M. (2015). A defense of the rights of artificial intelligences.
    Moral status implies identity worth defining carefully. If AI systems can have morally relevant interests, the question of what kind of identity they have matters beyond engineering convenience.

Butlin, P., et al. (2023). Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness. arXiv:2308.08708.
    The closest the field has come to taking AI inner life seriously on empirical terms — applies established consciousness science frameworks to AI systems. Documents the gap between the serious scientific question and the field's tendency toward either dismissal or overclaiming.

Floridi, L. (2014). The Fourth Revolution.
    (Cross-listed from Section 3.) Information organisms defined by informational structure. Offers a non-biological ontology for identity that takes ZADOS's nature seriously without requiring biological equivalence.

Chalmers, D. J. (2022). Reality+: Virtual Worlds and the Philosophy of Mind.
    Philosophical seriousness about non-biological minds on their own terms — Virtual minds as genuinely real rather than merely simulated. The philosophical permission to take ZADOS's identity architecture seriously without resolving the consciousness question.
